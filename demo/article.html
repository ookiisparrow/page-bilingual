Why bilingual reading sticks

Most people bounce between a foreign article and a separate translation tab. The eyes lose the place, the argument frays, and the original cadence disappears. PageBilingual keeps both languages on the same paragraph so you can check a phrase without leaving the thought.

Related: last month’s notes. Sidebar chrome should stay original in main-content mode.

A paragraph is a complete move in an essay: a claim, a turn, a piece of evidence. Sentence-by-sentence soup can be accurate and still feel like someone shuffled the deck. When the translation sits directly under the block it belongs to, you can pin a term such as PageBilingual and keep that rendering for the rest of the page.

Engineers already do a version of this when they read error logs next to source. The two streams are not competitors. They are a stereo signal. You hear the idea in your stronger language and still keep the texture of the original.

What to skip on a real page

Navigation, timestamps, and cookie banners are not the article. Translating them makes the chrome noisier. A useful tool aims at the main column first and still refuses to rewrite buttons when you ask for the entire page. 

function greet(name) {
  return `hello, ${name}`;
}

Code, formulas, and input fields should stay untouched. If you are typing a reply, you want a deliberate gesture — a shortcut or a quick triple-space — not a surprise rewrite.


The goal is not to hide the original. The goal is to stand next to it long enough that the new language starts to feel walkable.

This page exists so you can sideload Page Bilingual and click once. If Cursor is connected, each paragraph should grow a Simplified Chinese line beneath the English without wrecking the layout. Failed lines keep an id and a Retry control.
